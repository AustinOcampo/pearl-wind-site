Why we built Pearl Wind

I spent my career working on fraud inside three Fortune 50 companies. My brother and cofounder is a security engineer. Even with that background, he was fooled by a convincing fake Vuori store. He entered his information, placed an order, and nothing ever arrived.

If a security engineer can mistake a fake store for the real thing, it says a lot about how convincing these sites have become. Vuori now maintains an official warning about fraudulent websites impersonating its brand.

We started investigating fake online stores on nights and weekends, expecting to find scattered copycats.

Instead, we found connected networks.

Creating a convincing fake store is now cheap and fast. Scammers can copy a brand's products and photos, launch a disposable website, advertise a massive discount, and begin taking orders before the real brand even knows the site exists.

The problem often happens outside the brand's own website - in social ads, search results, marketplaces, and messages. The customer sees the fake first.

And it's getting worse. Consumers reported losing $2.1 billion to scams that started on social media in 2025. Shopping scams were the most commonly reported type, and many began with ads leading to websites impersonating familiar brands and offering steep discounts.

The current model is built for whack-a-mole

Most brand protection treats every fake website as a separate case:

Find one. File a takedown. Repeat.

That may remove a single domain, but it doesn't explain where the other sites came from, which ones are connected, or why replacements keep appearing.

The brand is left paying for another dashboard, reviewing another queue of alerts, and assigning someone internally to manage it.

Cost to launch another fake = almost nothing
Cost to investigate and remove one = $100s

The economics favor the fraudster. For the price of removing one fake, they can launch dozens more. Any solution that fights this one domain at a time is losing by design.

We saw that limitation firsthand. In one investigation, a single fake led us to a 462-site shared-build network spanning dozens of different brands. The domains, products, and logos changed, but much of the machinery underneath was the same.

Treating those sites as 462 separate problems misses the bigger picture.

We do not stop at the domain

Once we confirm a fake, we treat it as a lead - not a finished case.

Our engineers take the site apart and examine the technical clues underneath it: the code it runs, the infrastructure supporting it, the services it depends on, and the commercial relationships keeping it alive.

Find. Verify. Connect. Act.

We find fake stores the brand may not know about. We verify they're actually fraudulent, so legitimate retailers don't get caught in bad takedowns. Then we go deeper to identify related sites, shared systems, and the larger operation behind them.

That allows brands to move beyond one-domain-at-a-time takedowns and take coordinated action against the network itself - whether the best pressure point is its hosting, storefront platform, advertising, or payment relationships.
